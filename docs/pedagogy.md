Good starting point — the -AT family is the classic entry into CVC word building. Here's the general progression once a child knows letter names/sounds:

**1. Word families (rimes) before random CVC words**
Start with one rime and cycle the onset — cat, hat, mat, sat, bat, rat, fat, pat. This is exactly what you're doing with -AT. The child only has to swap one sound each time, so the cognitive load is low and they start noticing the pattern (blend onset + rime) rather than sounding out letter-by-letter.

**2. Stay in short-a for a bit, then branch to other short vowels**
After -AT, move to other short-a families before jumping vowels: -AN (man, can, fan), -AP (cap, map, tap), -AG (bag, tag, rag). This reinforces that "a" reliably says /a/ across contexts, before you complicate things with a new vowel sound.

**3. Introduce one new short vowel family at a time**
-IT/-IG (short i), -OT/-OG (short o), -UT/-UG (short u), -ET/-EN (short e). Same rime-family approach each time. Order doesn't have to be alphabetical — many programs go a → i → o → u → e because a and i are the most visually/auditorily distinct, reducing confusion.

**4. Mix vowels within known families**
Once 2-3 vowel families are solid, start mixing them in the same session (cat vs. cut vs. cot) so the child practices actually attending to the vowel rather than guessing from word shape.

**5. Move from rime-blending to full left-to-right blending**
Shift from "onset + rime" to sounding out every phoneme (c-a-t rather than c-"at"). This is the bridge to decoding words that aren't in a family they've memorized the pattern for.

**6. Add consonant blends and digraphs**
-CVC with blends (flag, stop, trap), then digraphs (ship, chat, that, thin). Keep short vowels throughout — don't introduce long vowels yet.

**7. Long vowels / silent e and vowel teams**
Only after CVC blending is fluent: silent-e (cat → cake), then vowel teams (rain, boat, feet).

**8. Sight words woven in alongside, not instead**
A handful of high-frequency irregular words (the, said, was) taught by sight in parallel, since they don't follow phonics rules yet.
